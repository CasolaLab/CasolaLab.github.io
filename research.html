Research

Our overarching goal is to identify the genomic changes that generate new genes and new functions, and to determine how they contribute to adaptation and evolutionary innovation. Our work is primarily computational, and we collaborate with experimental laboratories to test the biological impact of the genes we identify.

De novo gene birth

De novo genes arise from previously noncoding DNA and have been associated with novel phenotypes in several lineages. However, the rate at which they emerge and the genomic features that favor their birth remain poorly understood in most clades. We develop computational approaches to identify de novo genes with high confidence, including synteny-based validation in whole-genome alignments and machine learning classifiers.

Key papers

    Noncanonical ORFs and microproteins

    Ribosome profiling and proteomics have revealed translated open reading frames outside and within annotated protein-coding genes, many of which encode microproteins. We participate in the TransCODE consortium to characterize noncanonical ORFs in humans and other organisms. We have recently shown that protein degradation might play a major role in regulating the expression of human noncanonical proteins and minor annotated isoforms.

    Key papers

      Transposable elements and gene domestication

      Transposable elements are a recurrent source of new host genes and regulatory sequences. We have documented the repeated domestication of transposase genes into cellular functions in insects, mammals and plants, and the ancient horizontal transfer of Penelope-like retroelements from arthropods to conifers.

      Key papers

        Gene family evolution and conifer genomics

        Conifers carry some of the largest genomes among plants. We showed that the Pinaceae experience elevated rates of gene turnover that are robust to incomplete gene annotation and highlighted the functional impact of gene family turnover in the Douglas-fir genome.

        Key papers

          Conifer drought tolerance genomics

          Drought is a major constraint on the growth of loblolly pine (Pinus taeda), the most economically important forest tree in the southeastern United States. We combine physiological measurements, transcriptomics and genotyping approaches to identify genes and regulatory networks involved in drought tolerance and to inform breeding programs aimed at improving productivity and resilience in this species.

          Key papers

            Convergent molecular evolution

            Convergent phenotypes offer natural replicates for identifying the genetic changes that underlie adaptation. We found that C4 grass lineages share more convergent amino acid changes across multiple chloroplast genes than their C3 relatives, extending molecular convergence in C4 plants beyond RuBisCO.
